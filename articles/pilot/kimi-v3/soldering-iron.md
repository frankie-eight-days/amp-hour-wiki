A soldering iron is a hand tool that melts solder to form electrical joints. Effective use depends on joint technique, thermal management, and tip selection matched to the thermal mass of the work: the standard technique is to heat pad and lead with the tip and feed solder to both, and thermal mass determines whether a low-power or high-wattage tool is required.[329][110] Hand-soldering tip temperatures of 700-800 degrees Fahrenheit are correct even though reflow profiles specify far lower peaks, because the profile describes mass reflow while a hand iron must compensate for heat conducted away from the joint; tip temperature is not joint temperature.[183] Temperature control is the threshold requirement for a usable iron: any temperature-controlled iron will do the same work, and the problem case is an abused iron that no longer melts solder reliably.[413]

## Joint technique

The standard joint technique is to heat the pad and lead with the tip and feed solder to both, rather than melting solder onto the tip first.[329] The characteristic beginner failure is attempting to hold component, board, iron, and solder simultaneously instead of preparing the work and relying on heat transfer.[413] Where an oversized component covers its pad, heat is applied to the lead so that the pad is heated through it.[488]

Flux is typically learned late, and neglecting it produces intermittent rather than obvious failures.[137]

Two irons are treated as the working minimum, because opposing irons on either end of a part allow it to be lifted off; the recommendation is stated flatly: one iron is not enough.[528][534]

## Thermal mass and tool selection

Thermal mass determines the required tool: desoldering older through-hole assemblies calls for a high-wattage iron, on the order of 100 watts.[110] Legacy tip-over-element irons perform adequately on low-thermal-mass work but not on high-thermal-mass joints, which is where cartridge tips are required.[716] One teaching lab keeps twenty legacy irons in service for instruction, low-thermal-mass student work being the case where old tip technology remains adequate.[716]

## Cartridge and induction systems

In cartridge systems the temperature is a property of the tip rather than of a controller, so the purchase selects a tip type and a temperature band; Thermaltronics encodes the band as a colour.[528] Fixed-temperature cartridge tips require owning an array of tips when several temperatures are needed, since there is no temperature control.[528] Cartridge and induction irons are driven by an RF amplifier and shut down in the cradle, reheating fast enough to be ready by the time the iron reaches the work.[528] Tip life and thermal performance are opposed design goals, set by the cladding material and the thermal resistance it adds, and manufacturers target one or the other: a long-life tip and a low-resistance, efficient tip cannot be had in the same part.[528] By 2021 cartridge tips had become the default for new purchases.[528]

## Purchasing guidance

The purchase decision is best framed as intended service life rather than price: a ten-year iron, a five-year iron, or a two-week iron.[288] For a buyer considering a 300-dollar iron, Weller rates as good while JBC sits at the top of the price range.[288] A high-wattage Hakko at roughly a thousand US dollars is not a hobbyist purchase.[606] A Metcal-clone Thermaltronics iron has been reported as a worthwhile above-average bench purchase.[606] The premium commanded by newer cartridge technology is worth paying specifically in high-throughput repair work.[384]

At the low end, a 30-dollar USB-powered portable iron was found to work acceptably.[633]

## Tool history

The Hakko 926 and 936 were long-lived bench standards; the FX888 succeeded them, and the analog knob-controlled model was discontinued in favour of the digital FX888D.[122]

## Skill and method independence

A soldering contest at Toorcamp was deliberately run on poor-quality irons, the point being to demonstrate results achievable with the worst available tool.[410] Surface-mount assembly is widely and wrongly assumed to require an iron, which makes it appear more intimidating than it is; solder-paste methods are substantially easier for newcomers.[454] Castellated edge headers only provide value to builders working with an iron rather than hot air, which is why some board designs omit them.[723]
