Shenzhen is the centre of an electronics manufacturing and component-trading ecosystem covering the Pearl River Delta, in which Shenzhen, Dongguan, Guangzhou and Zhuhai ring the estuary and a supplier for almost anything can be found inside that radius.[113] Its distinguishing feature is not labour cost but the ability to walk out and buy parts the same day, which is itself a competitive advantage over manufacturers located anywhere else.[80] At the centre of the ecosystem sits Huaqiangbei, a wholesale electronics market of thousands of small stands spread across six floors of some twenty buildings, with entire areas given over to a single category such as switch or connector samples.[125] The same density that makes parts available in hours also supports a large gray market in recycled, relabelled and counterfeit components, which is the principal hazard of buying there.[84][113]

## Geography, scale and history

Chinese cities are ranked in tiers that broadly predict what industry is present: Shenzhen and Beijing are tier one, while a large city such as Chengdu with some electronics distribution is tier three.[412] Figures putting 80 to 90 percent of the world's electronics in Shenzhen circulate widely but are hard to substantiate, because the share depends on whether the count is of finished consumer assemblies or of industrial equipment in which only the boards are Chinese.[582]

The transformation falls inside a single working career. An engineer who first visited in 1987 recalls that the car she rode in was the only private car she saw in the city.[424] Wage inflation was already running at about 13 percent a year while the region was still treated as the world's low-cost option.[111] The region also runs a shortage of qualified electrical engineers rather than a surplus, to the point that companies there outsource engineering work outward.[597] Board shipments continued out of Shenzhen through the COVID lockdowns with roughly one to two weeks of added delay rather than stopping.[587]

The city's population is highly migratory, and Chinese New Year empties it: roughly 80 to 90 percent of the population of a city of about 20 million leaves, and airports and train stations become impassable during what is the largest annual human migration.[414]

## Huaqiangbei and the component markets

The stalls are set up to serve manufacturers rather than individuals, but a hobbyist can walk in and buy small quantities from them anyway.[125] The orientation to business is what makes Shenzhen the place to run an electronics business; hobbyist-facing districts such as Tokyo's Akihabara serve a different customer and carry different stock.[245] Prices sit far below retail elsewhere: a GSM-capable smartwatch and a Starbucks breakfast both came to about $9.75 in the markets, and the same watch retailed for about $25 online.[321]

The market also trades in material that is not sold through any distributor. Reference books collecting mobile-phone schematics and register maps for widely used chipsets sell for around $10, including register sets that the silicon vendors themselves do not publish; the same material can be photocopied out of local libraries.[245] Cast-off assemblies from consumer products, such as iPad processor boards, are sold openly, and their substrates are far thinner than a standard board because they are the thin prepreg layers a multilayer stack-up is built from.[415]

Transactions work around the language barrier rather than through it. Stallholders generally do not speak English, but every stall has a calculator and everyone works from part numbers, so a purchase can be completed by exchanging a part number and a price.[121] Bringing a translator is the fastest way through the barrier, at the cost that people who always bring an assistant tend to spend years in the city without learning any Mandarin.[121] Without Chinese, working in Shenzhen is a genuine constraint rather than an inconvenience, because outside the expatriate circuit almost nobody speaks English.[669] A Chinese-language field guide to the markets, with the parts vocabulary printed in Mandarin so a buyer can point at it, is treated by regulars as basic kit for a buying trip.[654]

The advantage of the markets over any online catalogue is physical sampling: picking parts up and judging size, finish or opacity by hand, which is why buyers still travel rather than order remotely.[268]

## Services around the markets

Several services exist in Shenzhen at price points that have no equivalent elsewhere. A pogo-pin bed-of-nails test fixture with toggle clamps, the kind normally built in-house, costs on the order of $50 from stalls that do nothing else.[355] The procedure for ordering one is to carry the bare PCB to the stall and mark the holes to be contacted with a red marker; no Gerbers or drill files are asked for.[355] Hand-placement services exist in which an operator buys the stencil, populates and reflows a handful of boards, and is paid a day's wage for the job, which comes out roughly an order of magnitude cheaper than setting up a machine line for three to five boards.[341] An injection mould for a modest product enclosure cost roughly $20,000 in the United States against roughly $5,000 in Shenzhen.[218]

The ecosystem has also served volumes that most manufacturers will not quote at all. The distinguishing service offered to open-hardware projects was willingness to manufacture and fulfil batches of 100 to 1,000 units.[125] Hardware accelerators have operated in the city, flying founders out for a roughly twelve-week programme ending in a demo to investors.[120]

A common route into Chinese manufacturing is to find an existing similar product, in the markets or on a marketplace listing, and ask its maker to build a variant; the method breaks down only when nobody already makes something close.[255]

## Intermediaries, allocation and thresholds

Most listings on the large online marketplaces are trading companies rather than manufacturers — the Shenzhen term for a middleman who buys to order or on spec — so a hundred apparent sellers commonly front a single factory.[420] Because a product category usually has one actual maker, a buyer needing 3,000 units against 300 in stock has no second source, and a promised production run can still collapse when the maker cannot buy enough of its own microcontroller.[420] Buying at volume therefore effectively requires a local buyer who speaks Chinese and knows the manufacturers, because part selection is an iterative negotiation across interlocking constraints rather than a catalogue lookup.[420]

Access to silicon vendors is gated by volume, which is part of what pushes companies onto the market in the first place. A startup asking a major wireless vendor to open an account and speak to a field applications engineer was told that without a commitment of 250,000 units a year no assistance would be provided.[295] Early-stage companies without a recognised name struggle to get allocation from silicon vendors at all, which is what pushes them onto the Shenzhen spot market for parts.[437] Parts whose price does not fall with volume push buyers toward the gray and surplus markets by the same mechanism, and that is a common route by which counterfeits enter an otherwise careful supply chain.[81]

The threshold for taking production to the region at all is better expressed in annual revenue than unit count: around a million dollars a year for a mid-sized Shenzhen ODM, far below what a very large contract manufacturer would entertain, and below that figure the advantage goes to a plant close enough to drive to and watch.[544] One alternative to buying directly from an unknown Chinese supplier is to pay a national supplier a premium to source and rebadge the part, which buys an accountable local counterparty rather than the lowest price.[178]

## The gray market and counterfeits

Counterfeit parts span a range of origins: at worst a slug of metal in a marked package, more often dies that failed test on the genuine line, or authentic parts run on an unauthorised ghost shift and sold into the gray market.[113] Part-authentication schemes accordingly target a specific list of substitution modes — recycled components, unlicensed overproduction, test rejects, and outright clones.[186]

Several distinct patterns recur. Brand representation in the gray markets is loose enough that sellers will peel the labels off generic resistor reels and apply a name-brand sticker in front of the customer, then ask about fifty percent more for the rebranded part.[84] A part may carry the correct marking over an entirely different die: a part marked LM741 that is actually an LM317 regulator, so the failure has nothing to do with the number printed on it.[1] A counterfeit can also be a chip of the correct footprint carrying fresh labelling to make it look genuine, with X-ray of a suspect batch revealing an unrelated die inside.[157] Counterfeit electrolytics have been found as a large can containing a much smaller capacitor, so the part meets the mechanical footprint and nothing else.[5] Recycled parts sold as new arrive de-soldered with bent pins and solder bridges between them, evidence they were pulled off scrap boards on a hot plate, and are not worth the time to fit.[434] Even a part as cheap as the 555 timer is counterfeited and marked as a major manufacturer's product, because the marking costs almost nothing next to the premium the brand carries.[351] Consumables are counterfeited as well as silicon, with fake soldering-iron tips for popular irons widely sold through auction sites.[288]

Substitution reaches assembled boards as well as loose parts. An assembler substituting a surface-mount header with a cheaper plastic body produced connectors that melted and left pins at odd angles coming out of reflow, a substitution invisible until the boards came back.[239] A connector body that cannot take reflow will nonetheless survive hand soldering, because only one pin is heated at a time and the housing never comes up to temperature, so hand-built prototypes will not expose the defect.[239] Out-of-tolerance parts can reach shipped product unnoticed: a batch of nominal 0.1 percent resistors measured a couple of percent, found only when a later batch failed a range check after fifteen or more units had gone out.[22]

Price is the most reliable signal. A part listed around $1.50 at a Chinese catalogue distributor can be offered at $0.10 on a consumer marketplace, and that gap is itself the evidence the part is not genuine.[546] Cheap unbranded equivalents usually work well enough that buyers conclude they were fine, but they do not meet the genuine part's specification and tend to fail in the application where the margin mattered.[674]

Trading conditions during shortages amplify all of this. There is no established marketplace for reselling surplus semiconductors, so shortage-era stock changes hands through auction sites and brokers, which is exactly why parts bought that way are assumed to be fake.[384] A class of broker will accept an order for parts they do not hold and simply refund the money when they cannot deliver, so an accepted order is no evidence that stock exists.[600] Chips that were once catalogue items increasingly reach independent repair only through unvetted brokers who cold-contact shops, so every lot has to be tested before it goes near a customer's board.[507] When a chip has no supply at any price, repairers buy complete assemblies for the one part they need, scrapping an entire iPhone XR charging case to harvest its ISL9240.[507] Long-lived defence programmes still buy 1980s-vintage parts through brokers who source from individuals who stockpiled inventory decades earlier, which is the path by which counterfeits have reached aircraft such as the F-15.[186]

## Verification and defences

The standard defence against unauthorised substitution is an approved vendor list: passives can be left to the factory as long as they meet specification, while for a small handful of components the acceptable vendors are named explicitly.[113] Contract manufacturers that guarantee against counterfeits do it by buying only through authorised distributors, and by refusing customer requests to source the same part more cheaply from consumer marketplaces.[243] For an instrument whose accuracy is the product, the deliberate choice may be all-genuine parts and local assembly at higher cost, because the cheaper gray-market route yields fake parts that a precision design cannot tolerate.[184]

How much verification a part needs scales with how hard it is to verify. An LED is tested by whether it lights, while a microcontroller can be wrong in ways no quick incoming check will catch.[79] Marking detail is what incoming inspection is for: a part branded as Infineon was caught because the manufacturer's name was misspelled by a single letter.[595] A pin- and code-compatible clone of a popular microcontroller is a counterfeit even when it functions correctly, and whether that matters is an application decision: tolerable in a novelty product, not in something whose failure has consequences.[595] Finished goods can be checked the same way, by taking a product such as an SSD apart to confirm the parts look legitimate before committing anything important to it.[84]

Buying in the market in person is itself sometimes chosen as a defence. Suspecting that his distributors were simply buying in the Chinese market and reselling, one manufacturer concluded he would rather buy there himself, where parts can at least be inspected at the point of purchase instead of after shipping.[429]

## Copying and replication

Shenzhen factories can copy a consumer product from its promotional video within days: a selfie stick built into a phone case was on sale on a wholesale marketplace at a fifth to a tenth of the creator's price before his thirty-day crowdfunding campaign had closed.[321] Purely physical open-hardware products are readily replicated by Chinese manufacturers, sometimes at higher build quality than the original, which is the dynamic that undercut MakerBot and 3D Robotics.[327]

## Factory practice and local norms

Chinese factories span an enormous range, from a cell where eight robots assemble a mouse around a lazy Susan with no human involvement to a third-floor walk-up with unlit stairwells where the wave-solder operator works in heels; seeing both ends is what teaches a buyer what is normal.[450] A local project manager with manufacturing experience earns his keep less as a translator than as a calibrator, able to say whether what the factory has just proposed is outrageous or simply normal practice in Shenzhen.[450]

Where labour is cheap relative to tooling, a factory will solve a part problem with people rather than a new mould: 5,000 RJ45 cables had their moulded locking-tab guards trimmed off by hand with a knife because that cost less than recutting the compression mould.[450] Commodity-looking items shipped with a Chinese-built product, such as a short patch cable, are typically made to order rather than pulled from a shelf, so a change request is a change to somebody's process rather than a different catalogue number.[450]
