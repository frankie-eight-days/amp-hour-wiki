Working with Chinese manufacturers is the set of commercial and engineering practices by which a hardware company outsources production to a contract manufacturer in China, most commonly in the Pearl River Delta. The decisive advantage is not labour cost but supply-chain density: components and vendor capabilities for almost anything can be found within a single region, often within a short drive of the assembly line.[113][175] The corresponding costs are managerial rather than technical — selecting a factory, holding quality across reorders, controlling tooling and intellectual property, and maintaining enough physical presence to close the feedback loop.[60][279][295] Below roughly a thousand to five thousand units the arrangement generally does not pay for itself, because factories make their margin on volume and are hard to interest at smaller quantities.[113]

## Volume thresholds

Factory interest is a function of volume, and the thresholds are well defined. Under roughly a thousand units the labour saving is small relative to the overhead of managing production in China, and domestic manufacture buys better intellectual-property protection and a faster design-build-test cycle.[113] Serious interest begins around five thousand units, because factories earn their money on the labour component and markup that volume supplies, and attention below that figure is genuinely hard to obtain.[113] A couple of thousand units is small volume by Chinese standards, close to the point where factories will not engage, and at such quantities the tooling rather than the assembly is what makes the run expensive.[173] An existing relationship does not transfer downward: a contract manufacturer that knows a customer from hundreds-of-thousands-unit programmes will not be interested in a few thousand units spread across five items.[173]

For a foreigner engaging Chinese factories directly, a hundred units is the practical inflection point — the minimum order at which a factory will talk to you at all, still smaller than it wants, accepted on the expectation that you will grow, and the quantity at which dinners with factory owners begin.[414] At that volume the cheaper path is often the white-label showroom: asking to modify something the factory already builds rather than commissioning a design from scratch.[414] Component vendors set their own threshold higher still, wanting to hear a path to roughly a hundred thousand units before they engage, which makes vendor relationships difficult for a company manufacturing at a few tens of thousands a year.[475]

Between these thresholds lies a well-recognised gap. A project at around 2,500 units can be stranded in a no-man's-land: too many to build by hand over weekends, too few to attract a contract manufacturer the company would trust with the whole job.[350] The same zone catches crowdfunded projects that raise enough to make garage assembly impossible but not enough to buy their way into a factory's attention.[175] One widely circulated rule of thumb holds that manufacturing in China should not be attempted without roughly a hundred thousand dollars behind the project, an argument made mainly on the cost of protecting intellectual property.[434]

## Selecting a manufacturer

Contract manufacturers are conventionally classified by revenue, where revenue means the cost of goods sold of each product multiplied by its volume and summed across product lines: tier one at a billion dollars or more, tier two between $250 million and a billion, and tier three below $250 million.[451] The tiers are not a quality ranking but a matching problem — tier-three plants operate on lower margins and are more nimble for lacking the overhead, and the right choice depends on the volume and on what capability the product actually needs.[451] Advice to use the same tier-one manufacturer as a major consumer brand is usually a poor decision for a small company, and tends to come from people expert in business who have never taken a physical product through production.[402]

Factories also have specialities that are not advertised, so matching a plant to what is actually being built is a real selection problem that first-time builders rarely know how to navigate.[363] A workable procedure is to assemble a candidate list, request quotes, narrow the field on those quotes, and then visit, since a factory that believes the customer is serious will readily host a tour.[280] The most reliable route to a first manufacturer, however, is a referral from someone who has already shipped with them, which substitutes their filtering for the buyer's own trial and error.[161] On open marketplaces the selection heuristic that works is not lowest price but evidence that the supplier will do a good job; the cheapest quote is the one to discard.[607] Custom cable assemblies and similar parts are easy to obtain by finding a supplier already making something similar and asking for specific changes — the difficulty is low volume, not customisation.[534]

Some smaller manufacturing houses invest free engineering iterations in startups they judge promising, working on a project for a number of cycles without charge in exchange for a position in the company's future volume.[363] Contract manufacturers generally farm out enclosure design and board layout to outside specialists, because their competence is the assembly line rather than design.[114] Mechanical suppliers vary enormously in equipment, engineering skill and quality process, so a supplier that can technically make a part may still be unable to make it consistently; the electrical side is closer to binary, since a house either can place a given package or cannot.[437]

## Cost structure

Contract assembly economics are dominated by setup charges, which can run twenty to thirty dollars per board once amortised across a small run — more than the selling price of a simple low-volume board.[458] In the United States, placement runs about five to six cents per component, so a board with 500 parts carries several tens of dollars of placement cost alone, and the annual figure reaches hundreds of thousands of dollars at modest volume.[237] Assemblers ask customers to supply roughly ten percent excess on consigned parts, and about twenty percent on 0402 and smaller, to cover placement losses; expensive parts are excepted and covered only by enough for a few extra boards.[716] An order for a hundred boards is typically built as 105 to 107 and all of them tested, so that the customer receives a hundred boards that passed rather than a hundred that were attempted.[716]

Tooling is the largest single cost difference for plastic parts. A precision injection mould for a small part is quoted at roughly $40,000 to $200,000 in a high-cost country, against Chinese injection-mould non-recurring engineering quoted in the hundreds of dollars.[379] Tariffs can invert the intended incentive where they apply to raw components but not to finished goods: importing parts for domestic assembly attracts the duty while importing the completed product does not.[458]

One way to cost a product without access to Chinese pricing networks is to base the estimate on catalogue distributor pricing and local manufacture, apply a multiple of about two and a half to three, and treat any Chinese saving as upside rather than as the plan.[113] Outsourcing also carries a cash-flow cost distinct from unit cost: obtaining a volume price means buying several months of inventory in one lot, whereas in-house assembly allows building to order a few days ahead.[237] Even at prototype quantities of four or five boards, paying a contract manufacturer around twelve hundred dollars to source and assemble them can be the right call, and it doubles as a rehearsal of the handover a volume house will require.[445]

## Handover and design for manufacture

The common failure of first-time hardware companies is treating the factory as a black box that will turn a prototype into a product. Factories employ real engineers, but that is no guarantee they are better than the customer's, and handing a design over on faith is how projects go wrong.[60] The point at which startups typically engage a Chinese factory is the eighty percent point: a prototype that works for minutes, funding in place, and no idea how to select a factory without losing money, intellectual property, or a year.[113] A turnkey arrangement concentrates that risk, since the design, the money and the schedule all go to one contractor and there is no fallback if it fails to produce.[114] Blaming a vanished contract manufacturer is a standard excuse from failed crowdfunded projects; the alternatives are hiring someone whose business is managing the factory, or going over and supervising the build.[142]

Conventional contract manufacturing works by throwing the files over the fence and letting the house set up and run the job, which places the setup check with the party that does not know the design; requiring the customer to approve every part before the machine starts moves that check to the one person who does.[243] Once a design is sent out, the option of walking onto the floor to clarify a detail disappears, and every subsequent change is billable, because change orders are where the manufacturer's margin is made.[255] Design feedback is available if asked for — a manufacturer will call out problems such as vias buried under a QFN before the board is built, and mould designers will do the same for wall thickness and draft.[350] A reasonably sized house will also let a customer design around the parts already loaded on its machines, removing setup and sourcing cost from the build.[216]

What suppliers will not do is volunteer the full set of constraints up front. Contract manufacturers, prototype houses and component vendors do not answer the question of what will go wrong with what they are selling; the limits emerge from actually building.[229] A prototype house may quietly hand-solder a part to get a job out of the door and say nothing, so the process fails only when the order reaches a hundred thousand and the factory reports that it cannot be done — volunteering that kind of feedback is what separates a good contract manufacturer from a bad one.[229] For a first production batch it is almost always better to build somewhere the team can walk to and look at, because a first batch of a new product will have problems nobody has seen before.[314]

Documentation discipline on the customer's side matters independently of the factory's competence. A design ordered from a Chinese supplier but never documented internally can be lost entirely when the one person who knew the arrangement leaves; a major printer manufacturer reordered the same keypad for years with no design control over it.[716] Security steps delegated to the factory as a final production step are also liable to be missed: a shipping product from a major company was found with its debug interface unlocked because enabling the lock had been left to the contract manufacturer.[552]

## Quality control and substitution

Assembly houses stock the common passive values and will supply them from their own reels, which is cheaper than buying a full reel, but the substituted part carries whatever temperature coefficient and power rating the house happens to stock, so any passive with a real specification has to be called out explicitly.[17] More seriously, a factory may substitute capacitors or buy through a broker holding counterfeit stock because of its own supplier relationships; the resulting failures appear months later with thousands of units in the field, and tracing them backwards is far harder than preventing them.[60] Face-to-face contact is what marks a customer as serious to a factory, and most customers never introduce themselves, which is where much of the substitution risk originates.[60] A factory with impressive equipment may also quietly subcontract small orders to a cheaper shop and keep the margin, the failure mode being that it does not quality-check the shop it subcontracted to.[280]

The consequences of substitution can be subtle. On a large LED installation, a subcontracted cable assembly built with non-original connector housings had slightly smaller keying than the specified Molex part, which made it possible to plug the connector in reversed, and that one orientation killed the first LED driver in the chain.[412] The failure required several factors together — remote assembly, a substituted connector with weaker keying, and assembly workers with no reason to care whether it clicked in correctly — and a single protection resistor on the affected input would have made the combination harmless.[412]

Colour and finish are controlled with golden samples kept at the end of each production line, against which each batch is judged acceptable or not before it is fitted to shipping product.[363] Hand processes that replace automation need explicit process control to hold quality: soldering iron temperatures drift, so a written calibration procedure and worker training have to be imposed rather than assumed.[113] That is the price of the trade in which Chinese labour substitutes for automation, letting a company hand-solder wire harnesses and through-hole parts rather than spend non-recurring engineering to automate an immature design.[113]

Traceability is tier-dependent. A tier-one factory can trace an individual serial number back to the reel a component came from, and to the lot and time window of the solder paste used; that level of tracking is what allows a return driven by a bad component to be pinned down, and it is not available at tier three.[451] Where outgoing product would be compromised, stopping the line is the correct response, but it is a finite resource — used too often, the factory stops returning calls.[279] A line-down event is otherwise the worst outcome in contract manufacturing, because an idle line costs money without producing any, which is why companies will pull an engineer off holiday and fly them out to clear it.[474]

Quality also drifts with time rather than only with individual decisions. Contract manufacturers earn their margin over the life of a programme and often make it on the back end, so a relationship that has settled is exactly where drift appears.[328] The counter is periodic presence: visiting to confirm the line has not been relocated to the basement and that the job still has priority, precisely when everything appears to be running smoothly.[328]

## Presence and communication

Whether production can be run remotely depends on the product and on the customer's own track record. For a complex consumer product a beachhead in China is close to a precondition, partly because the time difference leaves only two overlap windows a day.[113] Producing in China without a dedicated Chinese-speaking person on the ground goes badly even for those with prior manufacturing experience, and volume does not automatically imply China, since capable partners exist in western Europe.[295] Flying in produces a burst of progress followed by a stall: a visitor gets a great deal done in a week, and when he leaves momentum stops, because physical presence is what closes the question-and-answer loop in real time.[113]

The mechanism behind that effect is authority as much as bandwidth. Factories are accustomed to remote design teams and their attendant delay, so a designer physically on site who can authorise a change becomes an unusually valuable resource and is treated accordingly.[279] A designer on the floor with decision authority can close changes in an afternoon that would otherwise take days or weeks, which is why sending a proxy produces a materially different result.[279] Component problems can usually be resolved within a day or two by getting the local manufacturer's representative to the factory, a level of vendor support not available at the same speed elsewhere.[279]

Trip counts are correspondingly high. Bringing one consumer product through a Chinese factory took ten to twelve trips, which stops being enjoyable after the first two or three, and attempting the same project hands-off is described as impossible even for simple products.[175] Shipping consumer hardware at scale has meant months at a stretch camped on the assembly line, alongside selecting the manufacturer, setting up the supply chain, and attending the relationship dinners factory owners expect.[437] A more economical cadence used on one long-running consumer programme is roughly three trips a year, batched so that the responsible engineer is physically present every time an injection-moulding tool is opened, which is the moment where a mistake is most expensive to discover late.[414]

Ramp is staged deliberately rather than handed over at once: the first builds are run by the design engineers, after which other people are trained on the product so they can answer problems on the line, because a handful of designers cannot remain in China indefinitely.[363]

Several practical conventions attach to factory travel. The most effective problem-resolution tool is a camera good enough to photograph an actual defect, circle it, and email it to everyone at once, since a conference room rarely has a microscope and a photograph settles the argument.[279] Everything needed should fit in carry-on, because checked luggage arriving a day late costs a day of a trip that exists to compress the feedback loop.[279] Small personal consumables matter, as locally supplied gloves and finger cots are sized for local hands.[279] Buying supplies locally can cost half a day to a market by taxi with a language barrier at the far end, so experienced visitors carry what they expect to need.[279] For custom parts, the fastest way through a supplier's drawing conventions is to ask for a drawing in whatever format the supplier already uses and mark it up, rather than producing documentation in the customer's own format.[607]

## Supply chain, tooling and lock-in

The reason ramping quickly is possible in China is that components are made next door to the assembly, whereas nobody can overnight ten million parts from a catalogue distributor.[363] Chinese manufacturing has moved from taking Western designs to vertical integration, with camera modules, cables and enclosures for one product assembled in neighbouring buildings, so that almost any part can be had immediately.[437] Custom machined and gold-plated parts that would require finding and qualifying vendors across an entire country elsewhere sit within about half an hour of the final assembly factory, which is what compresses the iteration loop.[175]

Tooling, however, creates lock-in. Injection moulds are usually held by the manufacturer that cut them, so a plastic part cannot be moved to another supplier on price without paying for tooling a second time.[541] A supplier that tools up for a customer who is already in a bind acquires pricing power: with the tooling paid for and sitting in their plant, re-tooling elsewhere costs more than the premium, so the premium is paid indefinitely.[178]

Component continuity is a related discipline. A larger contract manufacturer employs staff whose job is supply-chain continuity, and their deliverable is a set of qualified alternates named in the design so that no jelly-bean part is single sourced.[279] At scale a manufacturer may need to build a line or open a factory to meet demand, which takes long enough that single-sourced components have to be fixed early rather than during a fast development cycle.[279] When a platform-defining part goes obsolete with no last-time buy, the sequence is to buy up world stock and then commission a manufacturer to copy the design and supply a compatible part, because changing the part would strand every module already built around it.[178] A copied part is never an exact duplicate and has to be fully re-characterised: on one such programme, automated insertion jigs were built to mate the replacement connectors a thousand times at varying angles and measure gold wear and expected life.[178]

Scheduling has a fixed annual constraint. Chinese New Year shuts suppliers down for something like two weeks, and orders placed just before it are accepted and then shipped whenever the plant restarts.[79] Scheduling around it is routine practice: work involving Chinese suppliers is simply not started in late January, because the shutdown has been planned around for months at the other end.[477]

For a seasonal consumer product the schedule constraint is absolute. Sales are exponential around Christmas, competitors' advertising has already set what children ask for, and every week of lateness removes the tall part of the curve.[113]

## Intellectual property

The realistic intellectual-property risk is not abstract theft but a design being produced alongside its own order, either at another factory or on a night shift in the same one.[295] Absent a specific and binding contractual prohibition, a Chinese manufacturer copying the product it builds is not in itself unlawful, and small customers rarely have the leverage or legal resources to impose such a contract.[524] Protection is therefore a matter of doing a few specific things differently, including registering a Chinese trademark, rather than relying on the NDA-style paperwork used elsewhere.[524] The publicised risk case is a highly funded crowdfunding project that goes to a Chinese manufacturer for cheap assembly and finds the product on sale before its own launch, though how often this happens is less clear than the anecdotes suggest.[524]

## Alternatives and boundaries

Domestic or in-house manufacture remains the better choice at low volume, both for intellectual-property protection and for cycle time.[113] Choosing assembly equipment to bring production in-house is itself a due-diligence problem: the checks that matter are running the actual board on the actual machine and selecting a machine that working contract manufacturers use in daily production, rather than relying on a salesperson's account.[237] Sourcing can also be split by process economics rather than moved wholesale — a one-person instrument business may buy CNC-machined aluminium enclosures from China because the domestic price is prohibitive, while hand-assembling boards in batches of about fifty at home.[643]

Hiring people who have taken products through Chinese production before does not make a company successful, but it reliably reduces the number of avoidable mistakes, which is where most crowdfunded hardware projects die.[402]

The advantages are specific to manufacturing and do not extend to engineering headcount. Altium relocated its headquarters to China and gave existing staff the choice of moving or finding another job.[593] The move was made on the expectation of low-cost, high-quality software engineering there, which did not materialise — a caution that applies to engineering organisations rather than to production.[597]
