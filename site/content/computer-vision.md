**Computer vision** is the field of extracting information from images and video by machine, encompassing stages from low-level filtering through object detection to semantic interpretation of what the detected objects mean, with each stage adding computation on top of the last.<sup><a href="#ref-254" title="Ep 254: An Interview with Andreas Olofsson - Adapteva's Ampliative Abacus (June 16, 2015)">[254]</a></sup> Its practical significance in embedded and robotic systems grew as hardware and optimized software made object detection—which was historically computationally intensive enough to require powerful CPUs or GPUs—feasible on small devices.<sup><a href="#ref-675" title="Ep 675: Changing Course with Shawn Hymel (August 8, 2024)">[675]</a></sup> The field spans classical algorithmic techniques such as optical character recognition and line detection as well as learned neural inference, with the two often performing complementary jobs on the same device.<sup><a href="#ref-531" title="Ep 531: Footprints and Symbols with Natasha Baker (February 21, 2021)">[531]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup>

## Task structure

A vision workload is not merely frame capture. It decomposes into filtering, object detection, and making sense of the detected objects, with each successive stage adding compute.<sup><a href="#ref-254" title="Ep 254: An Interview with Andreas Olofsson - Adapteva's Ampliative Abacus (June 16, 2015)">[254]</a></sup> The output of a vision system can take very different forms depending on what downstream consumers need. A basic classification system emits only a label indicating that an object is present in a frame; spatial perception goes further, reporting an object's distance and trajectory in the physical world, such as its position in meters along with its direction of travel.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> A spatial-AI camera module can therefore deliver object identity plus XYZ coordinates in meters as metadata, letting a downstream motor controller receive coordinates rather than pixels.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup>

Classical computer vision and neural network inference perform distinct jobs within one device: the network supplies object identity, while classical routines supply functions such as real-time lossless digital zoom and feature tracking.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> Classical techniques alone can be sufficient for structured problems; a schematic symbol builder, for example, was constructed from optical character recognition plus simple line and table detection applied to a highlighted region of a datasheet pin table, with no learned model required, and additionally infers the electrical type of each pin—input, output, or power—so the generated symbol is usable.<sup><a href="#ref-531" title="Ep 531: Footprints and Symbols with Natasha Baker (February 21, 2021)">[531]</a></sup>

## Hardware platforms

The choice of processing platform for vision is governed by the size of the software stack and the throughput demanded. Vision workloads mark the dividing line at which a Linux-class board beats hand-optimizing a tiny microcontroller, because a part with 4 KB of flash cannot hold the required software stack at any reasonable engineering cost.<sup><a href="#ref-267" title="Ep 267: Standing With Ahmed (September 16, 2015)">[267]</a></sup> Choosing a minimal processor and hand-writing assembly for a vision-equipped robot purely as an engineering challenge is considered poor engineering when a capable application processor is available.<sup><a href="#ref-267" title="Ep 267: Standing With Ahmed (September 16, 2015)">[267]</a></sup>

Field-programmable gate arrays occupy a distinct position. Low-cost FPGA boards made it plausible to put high-end vision on a drone for roughly fifty dollars, addressing problems that would stymie non-FPGA solutions.<sup><a href="#ref-302" title="Ep 302: An Interview with Clint Cole of Digilent (June 8, 2016)">[302]</a></sup> An FPGA placed directly behind the image sensor can perform real-time feature extraction and motion detection and hand only the results to a downstream processing board, turning the camera into a smart peripheral.<sup><a href="#ref-302" title="Ep 302: An Interview with Clint Cole of Digilent (June 8, 2016)">[302]</a></sup> Vision algorithms also motivate programmable logic on silicon-design grounds: fixed-function codec blocks are the right answer for a settled standard such as H.264 because no programmable engine will beat a dedicated IP block or ASIC on speed, whereas imaging and vision algorithms are unstandardized and still changing, favoring programmability.<sup><a href="#ref-254" title="Ep 254: An Interview with Andreas Olofsson - Adapteva's Ampliative Abacus (June 16, 2015)">[254]</a></sup> Programmability in a video pipeline earns its keep mainly when multiple standards must be supported or a standard is still in flux, which is secondary to raw throughput.<sup><a href="#ref-254" title="Ep 254: An Interview with Andreas Olofsson - Adapteva's Ampliative Abacus (June 16, 2015)">[254]</a></sup> Computer vision and intelligent sensing served as the beachhead market for at least one new FPGA family, specifically industrial and other cameras that fuse multiple sensing modes onto one platform, and smaller vision customers had previously been shut out of FPGAs not by capability but by price, cost, and power.<sup><a href="#ref-535" title="Ep 535: Efinix FPGAs with Sammy Cheung (March 21, 2021)">[535]</a></sup>

Application processors and heterogeneous SoCs form a third tier. NVIDIA's Jetson TK1, released around 2015, was the platform that made robot-mounted computer vision practical for hobbyists, and successor Tegra parts ended up inside AR headsets such as Magic Leap.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> An SoC pairing a DSP with a Cortex-M33 lets vision processing be offloaded to the DSP rather than consuming the application core on pixel work.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> Application processors that pair a main core with a low-power secondary processor are aimed at always-watching vision products such as smart doorbells.<sup><a href="#ref-664" title="Ep 664: Simulating doors falling off (April 3, 2024)">[664]</a></sup> Earlier still, industrial machine-safety systems were built as DSP-based embedded computer vision with camera feedback as the sensing front end, well before general-purpose embedded CPUs could handle the load.<sup><a href="#ref-131" title="Ep 131: An Interview with Andrew Seddon - Necessary Networked Novelty (February 4, 2013)">[131]</a></sup>

## Edge deployment and the smart-camera pattern

Before on-device inference, deploying vision meant streaming video to a cloud service or to a human watcher, which made many applications economically intractable.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> The enabling change was the combination of faster hardware and optimized software that brought object detection to embedded devices; the frame-rate gap was stark, with a laptop handling object detection at 60 frames per second while a Raspberry Pi 4 a few years earlier managed roughly one frame per second, too slow to be useful.<sup><a href="#ref-675" title="Ep 675: Changing Course with Shawn Hymel (August 8, 2024)">[675]</a></sup> Local inference has since been demonstrated on a plain Arduino-class board with a black-and-white camera, which provides enough resolution and color depth to classify hand shapes for gesture recognition.<sup><a href="#ref-675" title="Ep 675: Changing Course with Shawn Hymel (August 8, 2024)">[675]</a></sup>

A recurring architectural pattern is the smart camera that emits results rather than video. One recurring module concept combines a camera with an FPGA or fast microcontroller running OpenCV, pre-programmed for generic tasks and exposing a simple output such as a motion-detected flag rather than a video stream.<sup><a href="#ref-428" title="Ep 428: Setting Fire To The Tracks (February 3, 2019)">[428]</a></sup> Because the vision result is a few bytes rather than a video stream, a perception module can hang off an 8-bit ATmega or an ESP32 over SPI instead of requiring a host computer.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> A packaged person sensor combines a camera and processor in one part and exposes only an I2C output reporting whether a person is in frame, which is all many designs actually need.<sup><a href="#ref-633" title="Ep 633: Engineering Optimization (May 22, 2023)">[633]</a></sup> Encoding a vision result as a single state byte with room for CRC—using a byte's 255 values to cover roughly 20 states of interest—lets a fleet of cameras report over a low-bandwidth LoRa link at kilobytes per second.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup>

Software support for this pattern includes OpenCV's DNN module, which runs inference for models trained in PyTorch or TensorFlow, and pairing it with OpenVINO yielded roughly tenfold speedups across a range of platforms.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> Structured training in Python and OpenCV can collapse the unknowns on a self-directed robotics vision project from roughly 95 percent to 40 percent, largely by making the remaining gaps searchable.<sup><a href="#ref-373" title="Ep 373: Pedantic or Andrantic (January 2, 2018)">[373]</a></sup> Vendors have also shipped camera drivers with packaged object-recognition vision software, so machine-builders could avoid spending months writing their own recognition code.<sup><a href="#ref-116" title="Ep 116: Distribution, Wozniak & Robots - Early Eight-bit Endgame (October 7, 2012)">[116]</a></sup>

## Robotics

Vision closes the loop in robotics because the goal is typically to land inside a tolerance envelope rather than to follow an exact trajectory; CNC machining, by contrast, demands precise control of every axis at every instant and cannot be served the same way.<sup><a href="#ref-438" title="Ep 438: An Interview with Bart Dring (April 14, 2019)">[438]</a></sup> A camera-based alternative to analytic kinematics is to command a motion, observe with a camera where the arm actually ended up, and let the mapping from command to observed position build up empirically.<sup><a href="#ref-344" title="Ep 344: Back Into The Swing Of Things">[344]</a></sup> A practical way to give a machine vision-based registration on raw stock is to apply printed fiducial stickers to the workpiece for the camera to locate.<sup><a href="#ref-208" title="Ep 208: An Interview With Nadya Peek - Gallant Gcode Gerontology (July 21, 2014)">[208]</a></sup>

Robot mechanics drift over service life as microcracks develop, so embedded sensors and actively compensating algorithms are needed rather than a fixed calibration.<sup><a href="#ref-618" title="Ep 618: Refrigerators and Robots with Amitabh Shrivastava (February 5, 2023)">[618]</a></sup> In warehouse picking, grasp success is usually verified with a camera rather than with force or tactile sensing in the gripper.<sup><a href="#ref-618" title="Ep 618: Refrigerators and Robots with Amitabh Shrivastava (February 5, 2023)">[618]</a></sup> Vision can also replace mechanical protection entirely: substituting camera-based obstacle avoidance for armour and bumpers trades bill-of-materials and mechanical work for software effort that someone still has to write.<sup><a href="#ref-510" title="Ep 510: Knob and Tube Wiring (September 28, 2020)">[510]</a></sup>

Robotic welding illustrates the application conditions: the job is defined by imperfect fit-up, since a human welder's skill is producing a sound weld from parts that do not meet as drawn.<sup><a href="#ref-495" title="Ep 495: An Interview with Eric Klein (June 7, 2020)">[495]</a></sup> The investment case for automating it opened when better computer vision, LIDAR-derived point clouds for geometry, and arms that were already cheap and getting cheaper converged.<sup><a href="#ref-495" title="Ep 495: An Interview with Eric Klein (June 7, 2020)">[495]</a></sup>

## Localization and mapping

Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) tracks the pose of one or more cameras, often fused with inertial measurement units, while simultaneously building a map of the surroundings.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> SLAM is only required if rendered graphics must stay anchored to real-world surfaces; a head-locked display that moves with the user's field of view needs none of it.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> On an all-day wearable, power consumption rather than algorithm availability is the binding constraint on cameras and SLAM, so each vision feature must justify its energy budget against its usability gain.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> A microcontroller-class system cannot realistically run full SLAM, but simple optical flow for tracking user translation is within reach on the same hardware.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> With a single-camera power budget the design choice is inward or outward: an inward camera gives blink detection and gaze input, an outward camera gives world tracking, and both cannot be afforded.<sup><a href="#ref-638" title="Ep 638: Building AR Headsets with Aedan Cullen (July 9, 2023)">[638]</a></sup> Early BeagleBoard vision projects paired the board with a Kinect through the LibFreenect driver to build 3D maps and run face recognition on flying platforms.<sup><a href="#ref-59" title="Ep 59: An Interview with Jeff Keyzer and Jason Kridner - Bonafide BeagleBoard Bionomics">[59]</a></sup>

## Applications

Hardware test robots can be driven by running OpenCV over a video feed or a camera pointed at the device screen, locating a UI element visually and then physically tapping it.<sup><a href="#ref-369" title="Ep 369: An Interview with Jason Huggins (November 26, 2017)">[369]</a></sup> Livestock health monitoring works by recording pens continuously and recognizing anatomical features such as ears, tails, and legs, then classifying animals as healthy or abnormal from those features.<sup><a href="#ref-515" title="Ep 515: Embedded Linux with Jay Carlson (November 1, 2020)">[515]</a></sup> Battery-powered outdoor vision such as a trail camera works by duty cycling—waking to capture a frame and returning to sleep—and typically needs a separate co-processor for the detection step.<sup><a href="#ref-565" title="Ep 565: Here for a reason (November 7, 2021)">[565]</a></sup> Rear-approach collisions motivate automated bicycle sensing because the rider is aware of the impending impact only about 20 percent of the time.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup>

In commercial fishing, the vision algorithms for counting fish and identifying species were already adequate; the blocker to deployment was never the models.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> The constraints were physical: underwater cameras sit around two kilometers from the vessel and RF is absorbed within about two feet of water, ruling out wireless video backhaul.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> Neither cable choice works for long subsea runs, since copper corrodes and fiber resists corrosion but cannot be repaired in the field, on top of consuming vessel space needed for catch.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup> On-device processing provides roughly a billion-to-one compression, turning incoming 10-gigabit-per-second video into an output of fish counts, sizes, and species, and once the payload is that small, results can be carried acoustically through water, which propagates kilometers easily and leaves room for many frequency-division-multiplexed channels in the audio spectrum.<sup><a href="#ref-517" title="Ep 517: Depth and AI with Brandon Gilles and Brian Weinstein (November 15, 2020)">[517]</a></sup>

### Scientific imaging

Connectomics imaging cuts a tissue sample into about 26,000 ultrathin sections carried on electron-transparent tape through an automated microscope, with roughly six months of continuous imaging to capture the full stack.<sup><a href="#ref-431" title="Ep 431: An Interview with Adam McCombs (February 24, 2019)">[431]</a></sup> Electron-microscope imaging of tissue requires staining with a heavy metal, osmium tetroxide, purely to create enough image contrast, which necessarily kills the sample.<sup><a href="#ref-431" title="Ep 431: An Interview with Adam McCombs (February 24, 2019)">[431]</a></sup> The sample is microwaved during staining so the cells die without changing shape, preserving the geometric connectivity that the downstream 3D reconstruction depends on.<sup><a href="#ref-431" title="Ep 431: An Interview with Adam McCombs (February 24, 2019)">[431]</a></sup>

## Automotive perception and its limits

Camera-based perception in vehicles exhibits characteristic failure modes. A common collision-avoidance false positive is a parked car directly ahead that the driver intends to steer around: the system reads the closing trajectory as an imminent crash because it has no model of the planned maneuver.<sup><a href="#ref-524" title="Ep 524: LEDs and EVs with Mike Harrison (January 3, 2021)">[524]</a></sup> A camera-only driver assistance system was fooled by a large printed advertisement of a face on the back of a bus, reading the image as a pedestrian stepping out.<sup><a href="#ref-524" title="Ep 524: LEDs and EVs with Mike Harrison (January 3, 2021)">[524]</a></sup> Camera-only perception also degrades in exactly the conditions where driving is hardest—at night and in rain—because there is no complementary ranging sensor to fall back on.<sup><a href="#ref-524" title="Ep 524: LEDs and EVs with Mike Harrison (January 3, 2021)">[524]</a></sup>

The choice between camera-only and LIDAR-augmented sensing is contested. One argument for camera-only autonomy is the existence proof that human drivers operate with two eyes and no ranging sensor, making LIDAR a stopgap for the period when the optics and processing are not yet good enough.<sup><a href="#ref-524" title="Ep 524: LEDs and EVs with Mike Harrison (January 3, 2021)">[524]</a></sup> The countervailing position holds that LIDAR is compelling for one-time high-detail street mapping, where a single drive produces a detailed 3D model, but that this does not make it the necessary sensing technology for the driving task itself.<sup><a href="#ref-374" title="Ep 374: An Interview with Claire (née 'Clifford') Wolf (January 7, 2018)">[374]</a></sup>
