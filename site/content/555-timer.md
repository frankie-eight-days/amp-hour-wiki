The 555 timer is an integrated circuit dating from 1970 that was still shipping more than a billion units a year four decades later, sustained by an enormous body of legacy designs and by low-end consumer products.<sup><a href="#ref-16" data-tip="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs" title="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs">[16]</a></sup> It is produced by so many manufacturers that it is one part a designer will never be unable to buy, which matters more than it sounds when every other line item on a bill of materials carries a lead time.<sup><a href="#ref-16" data-tip="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs" title="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs">[16]</a></sup> Its combination of near-universal availability, a price measured in cents, and a function simple enough to be understood in full has made it both a standard building block for oscillator and timing circuits and the canonical vehicle for teaching electronics.<sup><a href="#ref-16" data-tip="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs" title="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs">[16]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-373" data-tip="Ep 373: Pedantic or Andrantic (January 2, 2018)" title="Ep 373: Pedantic or Andrantic (January 2, 2018)">[373]</a></sup>

## Availability, price and supply

Universal second-sourcing makes the part a design property rather than merely a component: because so many manufacturers produce a version, its availability can be treated as a given during design.<sup><a href="#ref-16" data-tip="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs" title="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs">[16]</a></sup> Price sets the terms for any proposed replacement. An expensive 555 costs about ten cents, which is why modules built to replicate the same function at around a dollar struggle to justify themselves however elegant they are.<sup><a href="#ref-16" data-tip="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs" title="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs">[16]</a></sup>

Ubiquity combined with margin has a less welcome consequence. There is enough margin in what is probably the most ubiquitous chip in the world to make counterfeits worth producing, a useful reminder that a low unit price does not imply low counterfeit risk.<sup><a href="#ref-351" data-tip="Ep 351: The Automation Amish (July 10, 2017)" title="Ep 351: The Automation Amish (July 10, 2017)">[351]</a></sup>

During component shortages the usual direction of substitution can reverse. With specialised switching regulators unavailable and no space constraint on the board, building a switching circuit out of generic parts including a 555 becomes a serious option rather than a joke.<sup><a href="#ref-558" data-tip="Ep 558: Toasted Marshmallow Connectors (September 19, 2021)" title="Ep 558: Toasted Marshmallow Connectors (September 19, 2021)">[558]</a></sup>

## Internal design and variants

Parts carrying the same number from different manufacturers are entirely different inside. External behaviour is mostly standard, but there is no canonical internal design, which matters whenever a circuit depends on undocumented behaviour.<sup><a href="#ref-361" data-tip="Ep 361: An Interview with Ken Shirriff (September 25, 2017)" title="Ep 361: An Interview with Ken Shirriff (September 25, 2017)">[361]</a></sup> The die is simple enough that anyone can put it under a microscope and trace out the mask, so redesigning rather than copying is a deliberate choice; the plausible reasons are that each company believes its own engineers can do better, or simply that a fresh design is faster than re-taping someone else's mask.<sup><a href="#ref-361" data-tip="Ep 361: An Interview with Ken Shirriff (September 25, 2017)" title="Ep 361: An Interview with Ken Shirriff (September 25, 2017)">[361]</a></sup> The same divergence appears across the other jellybean parts: even the standard five-volt regulator was implemented differently by different manufacturers in the original era, so identical part numbers have never guaranteed identical silicon.<sup><a href="#ref-361" data-tip="Ep 361: An Interview with Ken Shirriff (September 25, 2017)" title="Ep 361: An Interview with Ken Shirriff (September 25, 2017)">[361]</a></sup>

The family includes variants aimed at different requirements, notably a CMOS version and a quad version, and the original designer was brought back decades later to produce a low-voltage redesign.<sup><a href="#ref-16" data-tip="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs" title="Ep 16: LED Designs, Last Minute Designs and Board Designs">[16]</a></sup> A low-power reimplementation showed what the architecture can reach when redesigned for the purpose, drawing 4.4 microamps at one volt while oscillating at 18 hertz, which places it in a different class of part from the original.<sup><a href="#ref-55" data-tip="Ep 55: Shonky Stiver Stultiloquence" title="Ep 55: Shonky Stiver Stultiloquence">[55]</a></sup> The same designer's other significant contribution was integrating an early class-D amplifier controller onto a chip; the concept already existed, and putting the controller on silicon is the part that mattered.<sup><a href="#ref-110" data-tip="Ep 110: Armstrong, Camenzind & Museums - Outstanding Oneirophoros Obituaries (August 26, 2012)" title="Ep 110: Armstrong, Camenzind & Museums - Outstanding Oneirophoros Obituaries (August 26, 2012)">[110]</a></sup>

Because the internal schematic was published in data books, showing every transistor, the circuit can be rebuilt from discrete transistors. One such reconstruction worked and became a kit that people solder together themselves.<sup><a href="#ref-609" data-tip="Ep 609: Open Circuits with Eric Schlaepfer and Windell Oskay (November 13, 2022)" title="Ep 609: Open Circuits with Eric Schlaepfer and Windell Oskay (November 13, 2022)">[609]</a></sup> The educational value of a discrete replica lies in pattern recognition: working through the internal structure teaches an engineer to see current mirrors, current sources and differential front ends as recognisable blocks rather than as an undifferentiated mass of components, which is the same skill that lets an experienced engineer read a schematic quickly.<sup><a href="#ref-213" data-tip="Ep 213: Travel Recaps and Altium Announcements - Artisinal Aussie Assemblage" title="Ep 213: Travel Recaps and Altium Announcements - Artisinal Aussie Assemblage">[213]</a></sup>

## Role in electronics education

The beginner sequence in electronics has not changed in about fifty years: build a small linear power supply, then timer circuits while experimenting with the component values, then op-amp and audio circuits. The move to microcontrollers has not displaced that starting point.<sup><a href="#ref-7" data-tip="Ep 7: Love Robots and Pantyhose Screens" title="Ep 7: Love Robots and Pantyhose Screens">[7]</a></sup> Blinking a light is the hardware equivalent of hello world, which is why a timer circuit is the canonical vehicle for teaching an entire board flow from schematic through layout to ordering the boards.<sup><a href="#ref-373" data-tip="Ep 373: Pedantic or Andrantic (January 2, 2018)" title="Ep 373: Pedantic or Andrantic (January 2, 2018)">[373]</a></sup> The same circuit remains a recommended first project for learning a new design tool, specifically because it is hardware-centric: there is almost no firmware to distract from the board work.<sup><a href="#ref-404" data-tip="Ep 404: Proof Of Blink (August 26, 2018)" title="Ep 404: Proof Of Blink (August 26, 2018)">[404]</a></sup>

One proposed pedagogical inversion is to start from the timer integrated circuit, which produces an oscillator immediately, then build the same function with an op-amp, and only then with discrete transistors. Textbooks generally run the other way, starting with transistors and working up, which delays any working result.<sup><a href="#ref-301" data-tip="Ep 301: The Nerd Calendar (June 1, 2016)" title="Ep 301: The Nerd Calendar (June 1, 2016)">[301]</a></sup> The specific failure that ordering exposes lies in the teaching material itself: generic transistor diagrams showing a base resistor with no value and no worked example, which leaves a learner with the topology but no way to calculate anything.<sup><a href="#ref-301" data-tip="Ep 301: The Nerd Calendar (June 1, 2016)" title="Ep 301: The Nerd Calendar (June 1, 2016)">[301]</a></sup> The rules of thumb that fill that gap are rarely written down — a base-emitter drop near 0.7 volts used to back-calculate node voltages rather than merely noted, about 0.2 volts for a Schottky, and rough current limits for common parts that remove the need to open a datasheet for routine decisions.<sup><a href="#ref-301" data-tip="Ep 301: The Nerd Calendar (June 1, 2016)" title="Ep 301: The Nerd Calendar (June 1, 2016)">[301]</a></sup>

## Timer versus microcontroller

Engineers reach for whatever is on the bench, so a microcontroller often gets used to blink an LED simply because one was already there. That is a reasonable choice when the person could have used the simpler part, and a problem when they could not.<sup><a href="#ref-166" data-tip="Ep 166: Prior Art, Wafer Fabs and Guns - Whimsical Wafer Waffling (October 7, 2013)" title="Ep 166: Prior Art, Wafer Fabs and Guns - Whimsical Wafer Waffling (October 7, 2013)">[166]</a></sup> The cost of the habit is heavy-handed solutions to very simple problems, arrived at without considering power consumption or the other constraints that would have pointed at a smaller part.<sup><a href="#ref-166" data-tip="Ep 166: Prior Art, Wafer Fabs and Guns - Whimsical Wafer Waffling (October 7, 2013)" title="Ep 166: Prior Art, Wafer Fabs and Guns - Whimsical Wafer Waffling (October 7, 2013)">[166]</a></sup> It remains genuinely odd to reach for a microcontroller or an Arduino to build a flasher when a timer does the same job, an argument that concerns teaching rather than efficiency.<sup><a href="#ref-171" data-tip="Ep 171: An Interview with Forrest Mims - Snell Solisequious Scientist (November 11, 2013)" title="Ep 171: An Interview with Forrest Mims - Snell Solisequious Scientist (November 11, 2013)">[171]</a></sup>

For a product rather than a demonstration the calculation reverses. Replacing a timer-based design with a microcontroller is usually correct, because reducing the bill of materials is the primary requirement after functionality itself.<sup><a href="#ref-40" data-tip="Ep 40: Adafruit, Chip heist, Hackerspaces - The Kit Conniption" title="Ep 40: Adafruit, Chip heist, Hackerspaces - The Kit Conniption">[40]</a></sup> The margin structure that requires is specific: parts costing around ten dollars in a product selling for forty. That multiple is what absorbs a competitor arriving or market conditions changing, and what makes selling through distributors possible at all.<sup><a href="#ref-40" data-tip="Ep 40: Adafruit, Chip heist, Hackerspaces - The Kit Conniption" title="Ep 40: Adafruit, Chip heist, Hackerspaces - The Kit Conniption">[40]</a></sup>

Replacing the part with a small microcontroller in the same package is an appealing idea blocked by a mundane obstacle: the power pin arrangement does not line up, so the drop-in substitution that would make it worthwhile is not available.<sup><a href="#ref-546" data-tip="Ep 546: Thousands Of Dependencies (June 21, 2021)" title="Ep 546: Thousands Of Dependencies (June 21, 2021)">[546]</a></sup>

## Applications

A marker of a strong applications engineer is finding an enormous number of uses for one simple part. That skill is learned in the field rather than taught, because no course produces it and it depends on wanting to see what a device can be pushed to do.<sup><a href="#ref-129" data-tip="Ep 129: An Interview with Brett Fox and Dr Jeroen Fonderie - Device Doubling Decretum (January 21, 2013)" title="Ep 129: An Interview with Brett Fox and Dr Jeroen Fonderie - Device Doubling Decretum (January 21, 2013)">[129]</a></sup>

In a portable recording preamplifier, a 555 drove a one-watt audio amplifier into the secondary of a surplus audio transformer to generate a few hundred volts from batteries. Running the oscillator well above the audio band kept the hum out of the recording and let small capacitors do the filtering, where electrolytics would not have fitted.<sup><a href="#ref-115" data-tip="Ep 115: An Interview with Dr Greg Charvat - Watcher of Wraithlike Walls (September 30, 2012)" title="Ep 115: An Interview with Dr Greg Charvat - Watcher of Wraithlike Walls (September 30, 2012)">[115]</a></sup> A more mundane but instructive use is the fifteen-minute timer in an ultraviolet eraser, which matters because leaving parts under the lamp indefinitely can shorten or end their life rather than simply erasing them faster.<sup><a href="#ref-68" data-tip="Ep 68: Radiation Chips & Old Package Types - Technocratic Toilet Troubleshooting" title="Ep 68: Radiation Chips & Old Package Types - Technocratic Toilet Troubleshooting">[68]</a></sup>

The part also appears in early professional work. One engineer's first paid job was built around it: a multi-channel converter taking a truck's 24-volt supply down to 12 volts to drive lighting, with a switchable mode that flashed one of the outputs.<sup><a href="#ref-555" data-tip="Ep 555: Timing is Everything (August 30, 2021)" title="Ep 555: Timing is Everything (August 30, 2021)">[555]</a></sup> The lesson from that job was commercial rather than technical — having charged only for the parts and then been paid several times that amount, the realisation that electronics could be a source of income arrived before any of the engineering did.<sup><a href="#ref-555" data-tip="Ep 555: Timing is Everything (August 30, 2021)" title="Ep 555: Timing is Everything (August 30, 2021)">[555]</a></sup>

## Design contests

A workable framework for running a design contest around a single part is to separate categories for art, for complex or extreme circuits, for minimalism where two components do something remarkable, and for utility including test equipment and computation.<sup><a href="#ref-27" data-tip="Ep 27: 555 Contest, Computer Museum, Octopart - The Green Pen Hornswoggle" title="Ep 27: 555 Contest, Computer Museum, Octopart - The Green Pen Hornswoggle">[27]</a></sup> What wins such a contest is novelty and documentation rather than technical difficulty. A modular set of blinking dominoes was electrically just a timer flashing a light and another receiving it; the application was what made it interesting.<sup><a href="#ref-197" data-tip="Ep 197: Spacing Out On Space - Dave's Dongle Designing (May 5, 2014)" title="Ep 197: Spacing Out On Space - Dave's Dongle Designing (May 5, 2014)">[197]</a></sup> The discouragement worth resisting is the assumption that some unseen competitor has been perfecting an entry for five years: the cleverest idea tends to win, and entries with poor documentation lose regardless of how good the circuit is.<sup><a href="#ref-197" data-tip="Ep 197: Spacing Out On Space - Dave's Dongle Designing (May 5, 2014)" title="Ep 197: Spacing Out On Space - Dave's Dongle Designing (May 5, 2014)">[197]</a></sup> One winning entry demonstrated breadth rather than depth, using around twenty-five separate circuits to drive servos and actuators with flexible bend sensors as the trigger input, so that physical movement varied the timing.<sup><a href="#ref-284" data-tip="Ep 284: An Interview with Great Scott (January 27, 2016)" title="Ep 284: An Interview with Great Scott (January 27, 2016)">[284]</a></sup>

## Generated and programmatic design

Describing a circuit in code rather than drawing it changes the workflow for standard blocks. Instead of opening the datasheet and working through the formulas to pick a resistor and capacitor, a designer calls a function for an astable configuration at the desired frequency and has the values generated.<sup><a href="#ref-469" data-tip="Ep 469: An Interview with Craig J Bishop (December 1, 2019)" title="Ep 469: An Interview with Craig J Bishop (December 1, 2019)">[469]</a></sup> The scalable consequence is generating many instances programmatically, such as a web page that emits a chain of timer circuits spelling out an arbitrary message. It is the same block-replication idea used in programmable logic, applied to a schematic.<sup><a href="#ref-482" data-tip="Ep 482: Shine A Light (March 1, 2020)" title="Ep 482: Shine A Light (March 1, 2020)">[482]</a></sup>
