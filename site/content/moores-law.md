Moore's Law is the observation that the density of integrated circuits — the amount of silicon function that fits in a given area at a given price — doubles on a regular cadence, commonly stated as every 18 months.<sup><a href="#ref-17" title="Ep 17: EE Movies, Part Rants and SPICE.">[17]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-550" title="Ep 550: Finishing Prototypes with Zack Freedman (July 18, 2021)">[550]</a></sup> The statement concerns transistor density and cost, not processor speed, and the speed reading is a common misquotation.<sup><a href="#ref-17" title="Ep 17: EE Movies, Part Rants and SPICE.">[17]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-550" title="Ep 550: Finishing Prototypes with Zack Freedman (July 18, 2021)">[550]</a></sup> The trend has functioned less as a physical law than as a self-fulfilling prophecy sustained by competitive pressure: each manufacturer invests to keep the doubling on schedule because a competitor otherwise will.<sup><a href="#ref-41" title="Ep 41: Contests, Ham Radio & TWIT.tv - Ham, Spam, Thank You Ma'am (May 4, 2011)">[41]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup> As scaling has slowed, the industry's response has shifted toward parallelism, die stacking, and chiplet-based heterogeneous integration.<sup><a href="#ref-297" title="Ep 297: An Interview with Jake Baker (May 4, 2016)">[297]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-499" title="Ep 499: Discussing Chiplets with Ming Zhang (July 5, 2020)">[499]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-704" title="Ep 704: Applied Embedded Electronics with Jerry Twomey (October 2, 2025)">[704]</a></sup>

## Statement of the law

The trend is usually quoted as a doubling every 18 months, though formulations of 18 to 24 months also circulate; its author co-founded the company named for integrated electronics that produced the first single-chip microprocessor.<sup><a href="#ref-17" title="Ep 17: EE Movies, Part Rants and SPICE.">[17]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-497" title="Ep 497: An Interview with Brock LaMeres (June 21, 2020)">[497]</a></sup> The underlying statement is about how much silicon function fits in the same area at the same price, not about how fast a chip runs.<sup><a href="#ref-17" title="Ep 17: EE Movies, Part Rants and SPICE.">[17]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-550" title="Ep 550: Finishing Prototypes with Zack Freedman (July 18, 2021)">[550]</a></sup>

Two readings of the law are in common use: a lithographic reading, under which features shrink and transistor counts double, and a macro reading, under which memory halves in price and doubles in capacity every nine to eighteen months; whether the law is considered "over" depends on which definition is applied.<sup><a href="#ref-297" title="Ep 297: An Interview with Jake Baker (May 4, 2016)">[297]</a></sup> Under the density reading, the manufacturer chooses how to spend each generation's surplus: more speed, lower power consumption for battery life, or more dies per wafer delivering the same function.<sup><a href="#ref-550" title="Ep 550: Finishing Prototypes with Zack Freedman (July 18, 2021)">[550]</a></sup>

Plotting the transistor counts of released processors against the predicted doubling line shows the actual data oscillating ahead of and behind the trend rather than tracking it smoothly.<sup><a href="#ref-484" title="Ep 484: Man Behind The Curtain (March 16, 2020)">[484]</a></sup>

## Historical development

Digital logic was built in bipolar technology until around 1980; bipolar was abandoned not because it stopped getting faster but because its power dissipation became intolerable, which drove the industry-wide switch to CMOS.<sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup> Companies that bet on gallium arsenide for speed lost out when CMOS became fast enough, a recurring pattern in which the incumbent process outruns an exotic alternative.<sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup>

Through the 1990s, clock frequency was the headline competitive axis in the megahertz and gigahertz races.<sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup> That axis ended well before density scaling did: desktop processors sat near three gigahertz for over a decade, and core count replaced frequency as the marketed specification.<sup><a href="#ref-61" title="Ep 61: Moore's Law, GaN and SiC devices - Gallimaufry GaN Gabble">[61]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup>

Using the chessboard-and-rice doubling parable as a yardstick, the counted generations of technology doubling passed the 32nd square around 2006, the point at which an exponential progression becomes visibly overwhelming.<sup><a href="#ref-195" title="Ep 195: Guns and Mobile Labs - Nuanced Nomadic Non-essentials (April 21, 2014)">[195]</a></sup>

### Forecasts of the end

Predictions of the law's breakdown have a long history. One widely circulated forecast placed the breakdown of transistor scaling in the 2020 to 2030 window.<sup><a href="#ref-61" title="Ep 61: Moore's Law, GaN and SiC devices - Gallimaufry GaN Gabble">[61]</a></sup> An IBM semiconductor research leader estimated on a Design Automation Conference panel that conventional semiconductor technology had about two more decades of runway.<sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup> Around eight-nanometer device dimensions, CMOS has been expected to require a fundamentally different approach — a boundary that had been predicted for roughly a decade without arriving.<sup><a href="#ref-228" title="Ep 228: An Interview with Shahriar from The Signal Path - Quisquous Quivering Quadripole (December 16, 2014)">[228]</a></sup> GlobalFoundries' decision to halt seven-nanometer development was a single-company capability decision rather than an industry ceiling, since TSMC continued developing that node.<sup><a href="#ref-406" title="Ep 406: Nerds In A Corner (September 9, 2018)">[406]</a></sup>

Extrapolating exponential doubling to the physical limit of one bit per atom yields an absolute ceiling of roughly 600 years, beyond which all reachable matter would be consumed as storage.<sup><a href="#ref-23" title="Ep 23: The Innovation Speculation">[23]</a></sup>

## Economic character

The doubling cadence is driven by commercial survival rather than technical enthusiasm. Fabs pursue each process generation under competitive pressure: if one company does not advance the node, a competitor will, which is why the whole industry marches in step.<sup><a href="#ref-41" title="Ep 41: Contests, Ham Radio & TWIT.tv - Ham, Spam, Thank You Ma'am (May 4, 2011)">[41]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-564" title="Ep 564: Pavlovian Cheapskates (October 31, 2021)">[564]</a></sup> The trend functions as a self-fulfilling prophecy in which each company invests to keep it on schedule because a rival otherwise will.<sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup> CMOS scaling continues because demand for storage, processing, and lower power funds it; without that economic pull, the technical progress would stop regardless of what physics permits.<sup><a href="#ref-228" title="Ep 228: An Interview with Shahriar from The Signal Path - Quisquous Quivering Quadripole (December 16, 2014)">[228]</a></sup>

The industry has largely moved to a fabless-plus-foundry structure, with Intel the prime remaining example of a vertically integrated device manufacturer.<sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup> At the 28- and 20-nanometer generations, the process physics became severe enough that EDA tool selection could no longer be separated from the choice of foundry, forcing collaborative development and heavy mathematical modeling.<sup><a href="#ref-99" title="Ep 99: An Interview with Steve Leibson - Impavid Ideopraxist Insider (June 10, 2012)">[99]</a></sup>

Large corporations pipeline product development around the process cadence, so each year's product is far enough along that an individual builder cannot match it.<sup><a href="#ref-61" title="Ep 61: Moore's Law, GaN and SiC devices - Gallimaufry GaN Gabble">[61]</a></sup> As the density curve flattened, a counterpart cost curve moved the opposite way, with semiconductor development cost rising as dimensions shrink.<sup><a href="#ref-553" title="Ep 553: Debunking with Shahriar (August 10, 2021)">[553]</a></sup> Only about two or three companies worldwide can run five- and three-nanometer CMOS processes — TSMC and Samsung, with Intel potentially reaching them.<sup><a href="#ref-553" title="Ep 553: Debunking with Shahriar (August 10, 2021)">[553]</a></sup> Proprietary process design kits carry legal overhead measured in 800-dollar-per-hour lawyer time, so only ideas a team is already confident in get explored; open PDKs lower the barrier to trying speculative architectures.<sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup>

Falling digital electronics cost also reset pricing in adjacent markets: an entry-level benchtop oscilloscope sat near 800 dollars for decades until Rigol halved the 1052E's price to 400 dollars in 2009.<sup><a href="#ref-293" title="Ep 293: Call In Show #4 (March 30, 2016)">[293]</a></sup>

## Physical and design constraints

Below the wavelength limit of deep-ultraviolet lithography, a single layer is decomposed into several coarser patterns exposed on top of one another to reach the fine resolution, a technique called multiple patterning.<sup><a href="#ref-553" title="Ep 553: Debunking with Shahriar (August 10, 2021)">[553]</a></sup> Multiple patterning removes layout freedom that older nodes allowed: gates can no longer be placed at arbitrary orientations, and everything on a layer must run in the same direction.<sup><a href="#ref-553" title="Ep 553: Debunking with Shahriar (August 10, 2021)">[553]</a></sup>

Bond pads do not shrink at anything like the rate transistors do, so on advanced nodes a small die becomes pad-limited rather than transistor-limited.<sup><a href="#ref-272" title="Ep 272: An Interview With Luke Beno of Analog.io (October 21, 2015)">[272]</a></sup> Modern node names are marketing labels rather than feature sizes: a so-called two-nanometer node contains no two-nanometer features, and physics limits real dimensions to roughly ten or eleven nanometers.<sup><a href="#ref-704" title="Ep 704: Applied Embedded Electronics with Jerry Twomey (October 2, 2025)">[704]</a></sup>

Power is a parallel constraint. Efficiency was historically bought by lowering supply rails alongside parallelization, but rail voltages have approached diode-drop levels, removing that lever.<sup><a href="#ref-704" title="Ep 704: Applied Embedded Electronics with Jerry Twomey (October 2, 2025)">[704]</a></sup> Packing more transistors into a small area raises power density and makes heat extraction a serious limit, and adding cores only helps if memory bandwidth can feed them.<sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup> Current compute scaling is limited by power and by DRAM cost rather than by lithography alone, pushing gains toward efficiency instead of raw capability.<sup><a href="#ref-722" title="Ep 722: AI Tooling with Matt Liberty and Luke Beno (April 22, 2026)">[722]</a></sup>

High-speed RF parts are typically not built on leading-edge CMOS at all but on silicon-germanium BiCMOS, where SiGe bipolar devices provide higher breakdown voltage for power handling and the on-die CMOS is a far older node used as a helper.<sup><a href="#ref-553" title="Ep 553: Debunking with Shahriar (August 10, 2021)">[553]</a></sup>

## Slowdown and its consequences

Transistor count per chip has kept roughly doubling, but what changed is how the transistors are usable: single-threaded performance hit a ceiling and core counts rose instead.<sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup> After roughly 40 years of hard scaling, present-day improvements come from more parallel processing rather than smaller transistors, and no successor technology with comparable breadth of application has emerged.<sup><a href="#ref-704" title="Ep 704: Applied Embedded Electronics with Jerry Twomey (October 2, 2025)">[704]</a></sup>

System performance and transistor scaling are separable: architectural techniques such as parallelism can deliver performance gains that run ahead of the process-scaling schedule.<sup><a href="#ref-17" title="Ep 17: EE Movies, Part Rants and SPICE.">[17]</a></sup> A common design habit, however, has been to accept slow compute-heavy tools on the assumption that future process generations will make them fast enough, deferring optimization work indefinitely.<sup><a href="#ref-19" title="Ep 19: CAD programs, Systems Design and Renewable Energy">[19]</a></sup> The historical default answer to a performance shortfall was to wait roughly 18 months for faster silicon; when that stops working, the remaining lever is spending engineering time optimizing code or turning it into dedicated hardware.<sup><a href="#ref-317" title="Ep 317: A Decoupled Episode (September 28, 2016)">[317]</a></sup> ASIC design methodology has not been rethought at hundred-fold scale increments the way software practice has; chips are still designed with late-1990s methods because process scaling supplied the speed gains instead.<sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup>

If hardware performance stays flat for years, multi-year investments in optimizing compilers and hand-coded assembly start paying off because the gains are not erased by the next chip.<sup><a href="#ref-84" title="Ep 84: An Interview with Bunnie Huang - Bunnie's Bibelot Bonification (February 27, 2012)">[84]</a></sup> Physics makes the slowdown of transistor scaling essentially inevitable, and longer cycle times give architectural ideas more room to prove viability; under a fast cadence, by contrast, innovation is compressed into a window of months, because a full-stack idea must show a compelling improvement before the next node makes it irrelevant.<sup><a href="#ref-84" title="Ep 84: An Interview with Bunnie Huang - Bunnie's Bibelot Bonification (February 27, 2012)">[84]</a></sup> Backward compatibility combined with steady process gains makes incremental speedups the rational business choice, which suppresses architectural risk-taking such as moving the processor next to the memory.<sup><a href="#ref-84" title="Ep 84: An Interview with Bunnie Huang - Bunnie's Bibelot Bonification (February 27, 2012)">[84]</a></sup>

When scaling stops, memory capacity per generation stops multiplying, removing the yearly specification jump manufacturers had relied on for new product cycles.<sup><a href="#ref-61" title="Ep 61: Moore's Law, GaN and SiC devices - Gallimaufry GaN Gabble">[61]</a></sup> A better strategy for a small hardware business than waiting for scaling to stall is to target niches large corporations serve badly.<sup><a href="#ref-61" title="Ep 61: Moore's Law, GaN and SiC devices - Gallimaufry GaN Gabble">[61]</a></sup> Correspondingly, the end of easy gains in general-purpose computing opens more silicon niches, making specialized chip startups more viable than during the era of uniform scaling.<sup><a href="#ref-672" title="Ep 672: Silicon Revolution with Matt Venn (June 30, 2024)">[672]</a></sup>

### Stacking and chiplets

When a process node runs out of headroom, effort shifts to packaging improvements until packaging itself becomes the limiting factor and a new node jump is made.<sup><a href="#ref-84" title="Ep 84: An Interview with Bunnie Huang - Bunnie's Bibelot Bonification (February 27, 2012)">[84]</a></sup> Once density gains in the plane stall, the industry response is vertical stacking of die — the same move a densely populated area makes when land runs out.<sup><a href="#ref-297" title="Ep 297: An Interview with Jake Baker (May 4, 2016)">[297]</a></sup> Chiplet and heterogeneous system integration is positioned as the successor enabler for semiconductor innovation as monolithic scaling reaches its end.<sup><a href="#ref-499" title="Ep 499: Discussing Chiplets with Ming Zhang (July 5, 2020)">[499]</a></sup>

Integrating everything onto one monolithic die remains the performance-optimal answer, providing roughly 13 metal layers, hundred-nanometer wire pitches, and the best energy efficiency, cost, and bandwidth; the trade is that monolithic integration abandons modularity entirely, producing a statically compiled blob with no reuse, whereas chiplets give hardware the equivalent of software libraries and linking.<sup><a href="#ref-650" title="Ep 650: Accessible ASICs with Andreas Olofsson (November 12, 2023)">[650]</a></sup>

### Custom and reconfigurable logic

Process headroom running out revived interest in programmable logic, restoring the long-standing tradeoff between FPGA custom logic and brute-force parallel processors.<sup><a href="#ref-296" title="Ep 296: Gotta Update My Dog (April 27, 2016)">[296]</a></sup> Slowing process gains likewise increased interest in open-source FPGA toolchains, because optimizing custom logic becomes worthwhile when free performance no longer arrives.<sup><a href="#ref-421" title="Ep 421: The Legend of Keyzermas (December 23, 2018)">[421]</a></sup> FPGAs offer software-style deployment timescales — worldwide in minutes rather than years — but pay a substantial silicon cost for that configurability.<sup><a href="#ref-501" title="Ep 501: Discussing the Open Source PDK with Tim Ansell (July 19, 2020)">[501]</a></sup>

Commodity ARM cores driven by smartphone volume became powerful enough that custom hardware such as FPGAs stopped being necessary for many embedded control applications.<sup><a href="#ref-105" title="Ep 105: An Interview with Chris Anderson - Deambulatory Daedal Drones (July 23, 2012)">[105]</a></sup> Analog preprocessing before conversion retains real niches at low power, low amplitude, and low sensitivity, but analog will not match digital scaling for integration, complexity, and processing throughput.<sup><a href="#ref-119" title="Ep 119: An Interview with Dr. Kent Lundberg - Luculent Linear Legacy (October 28, 2012)">[119]</a></sup>

The long-run trend in the cost of compute is downward toward zero, now driven by GPUs, TPUs, and algorithmic efficiency rather than transistor scaling alone, letting models that once needed a cluster run on local machines.<sup><a href="#ref-626" title="Ep 626: Intelligent Routing with Sergiy Nesterenko (April 2, 2023)">[626]</a></sup> Very large scale integration also remains the enabling substrate in its own right: modern phased arrays are practical because integration supplies the many data converters and wide data pipes that mechanically scanned radar could not.<sup><a href="#ref-729" title="Ep 729: The Terahertz Frontier with Greg Charvat of Teradar (July 22, 2026)">[729]</a></sup>
